Does ad spend actually predict sales?

Multiple regression across 200 markets — TV, Radio and Newspaper spend vs. Sales.

Radio0.189 · p < .001
TV0.046 · p < .001
Newspaper−0.001 · p = .86 (not significant)

TV and Radio spend both predict sales with near-certainty — but Newspaper spend doesn't, once TV and Radio are already in the model. The simple correlation between newspaper spend and sales turns out to be explained by markets that spend heavily everywhere, not by newspaper spend itself, which is the actual reason multi-channel attribution needs more than one channel looked at in isolation.

Excel Multiple regression LINEST Hypothesis testing
How I did this

Data: the classic "Advertising" teaching dataset (James, Witten, Hastie & Tibshirani, An Introduction to Statistical Learning) — 200 markets, TV/Radio/Newspaper spend in $000s vs. Sales in 000 units. Public dataset, no client or company data involved.

Method: built a multiple linear regression in Excel from scratch using LINEST/INDEX array formulas, cross-checked against the Data Analysis ToolPak's regression output. TV, Radio and Newspaper spend together explain 90% of the variation in sales across the 200 markets (R² = 0.897).

Result: TV (coefficient 0.046) and Radio (0.189) are both statistically significant predictors (p < .001) — every extra $1,000 spent on Radio drives roughly 4x more sales than the same $1,000 on TV. Newspaper's coefficient (−0.001, p = .86) means there's no evidence it moves sales at all once TV and Radio are accounted for, so cutting it should be trialled as a gradual reduction rather than an outright cut, to rule out any slower "halo" effect a single snapshot regression wouldn't catch.

Which customers are most likely to churn?

Segmentation across 7,043 telecom customers — screened seven fields, combined the two strongest.

Electronic check + month-to-month53.7%
Dataset average26.5%
Two-year contract customers2.8%

Customers on a month-to-month contract who pay by electronic check churn at 53.7% — roughly double the dataset's overall rate, and the highest of any segment tested. Contract length and payment method were the two strongest individual drivers out of seven fields screened, so I combined them into one view rather than crossing every field at once, which would have produced unreliable, tiny-sample combinations.

Excel PivotTables COUNTIFS / AVERAGEIFS Segmentation
How I did this

Data: the public Telco Customer Churn dataset — 7,043 customers, standard demographic/account/service fields. Public dataset, no client or company data involved.

Method: screened churn rate individually across contract length, payment method, partner status, dependants and tenure using PivotTables and COUNTIFS/AVERAGEIFS, then nested the two fields with the widest spread — contract length and payment method — into a single combined view.

Result: electronic-check customers on a month-to-month contract churn at 53.7%, against a 26.5% dataset average and 2.8% among two-year contract customers. Along the way I caught and corrected a common Excel mistake: averaging churn percentages across payment methods of different sizes gave a misleading "total" that didn't match the real, count-weighted figure — a reminder that a plausible-looking percentage still needs checking against the underlying counts.

Where is hospital capacity actually under strain?

Power BI dashboard over 318K admission records — which departments carry the greatest long-stay risk, and where is room capacity tightest.

Surgery has the highest long-stay rate of any department at 72.94% — but it isn't the department with the least room to spare. That's anaesthesia, at just 2.82 available rooms on average against a dataset-wide range of 2.82–3.27, which means the two departments worth watching for very different reasons aren't the same one.

Power BI DAX Power Query Data cleaning
How I did this

Result: surgery carries the highest long-stay risk (72.94% vs. a 65–73% range across departments), but anaesthesia is the most room-constrained (2.82 available rooms on average, the lowest of the five) — so the department most worth watching depends on which risk you're managing.

Data: the public "Healthcare Analytics II" hospital admissions dataset on Kaggle — 318K+ individual admission records across five departments, with fields for severity of illness, bed grade, ward type, available room capacity and length-of-stay banding. Public dataset, no client or company data involved.

Cleaning: in Power Query — checked column quality/completeness, corrected a type-detection bug where a bucketed range column was being auto-parsed as a date, and handled blank/null Bed Grade values explicitly rather than letting them drop silently from category counts. Set a custom sort order on Severity of Illness (Minor → Moderate → Extreme) via a hidden rank column, so every chart and drill-down reads in clinical order rather than alphabetically.

Dashboard elements & features: built two DAX measures — a Long Stay Rate (DIVIDE/CALCULATE over a >20-day threshold) and an average available-rooms figure — and broke both out by department rather than relying on raw patient counts, since department size varies enough that a simple volume chart is dominated by whichever department has the most patients rather than showing real risk. Colour-coding is driven by a single DAX measure mapping each department to a fixed colour via conditional formatting, so the same department reads the same colour across every chart regardless of how each one is sorted.
